♠♥♦♣ Contract bridge

Bridge Fundamentals

The ideas every bridge player uses, whatever system they play: how to value a hand, how many points you need for each contract, the principles behind bidding, and how to plan the play and the defence.

These are universal. Our own agreements (what each bid means) are in the System notes. When the Advisor writes "dummy points", "losers" or "total tricks", this page explains them.

Part 1 · Valuing a hand

High-card points (HCP)

CardPoints
Ace4
King3
Queen2
Jack1

The deck holds 40 HCP, so an average hand has 10. HCP are the starting point for every other count on this page.

Adjusting the count

  • Honours together are worth more. KQJ in one suit takes tricks; a K, Q and J in three different suits often doesn't.
  • Honours in your long suits are worth more than honours in short suits. AKJ65 is worth more than its 8 HCP; a singleton K is worth less than 3.
  • Short honours lose value: a singleton K, Q or J, and Qx/Jx, count a point less.
  • Aces and tens are undervalued by 4-3-2-1; queens and jacks are overvalued. A hand full of aces is better than its count.
  • Position: a K behind the player who bid that suit (you play after them) is likely to win; in front of them it is likely to lose.
  • Honours in the opponents' suit are good for defence and notrump, poor for playing in your own trump suit.

Hand shape

Shape is written longest suit first: 5-3-3-2 means a 5-card suit, two 3-card suits and a doubleton.

TypePatternsPlays best in
Balanced4-3-3-3, 4-4-3-2, 5-3-3-2Notrump, unless there is a major fit
Semi-balanced5-4-2-2, 6-3-2-2Either; depends on the fit and stoppers
Unbalancedanything with a singleton or void, or two long suitsA trump suit

Singleton = one card in a suit; void = none; doubleton = two.

Distribution points

Long and short suits take tricks that HCP don't count. There are two ways to add them. Use one or the other, never both for the same suit.

Length points (for the hand with the long suit)

Add 1 point for each card beyond 4 in a suit. ♠AKJ65 ♥K72 ♦43 ♣Q72 = 13 HCP + 1 = 14. Use length points when you open the bidding or when your suit is trumps.

Shortness points ("dummy points", for the supporting hand)

Once you have found a fit and will be dummy, short suits are where you ruff the declarer's losers. Add instead of length points:

ShortnessWith 4+ trumpsWith 3 trumps
Void53
Singleton32
Doubleton11

Example: partner opens 1♠ and you hold ♠Q872 ♥J95 ♦A10 ♣Q872: 9 HCP + 1 for the doubleton = 10 dummy points, a limit raise.

Without a fit, shortness is a weakness, not a strength: don't count it.

Rule of 20 and Rule of 15

Rule of 20 (first and second seat): add your HCP and the lengths of your two longest suits. With 20 or more, open. ♠AK953 ♥KJ842 ♦5 ♣73 = 11 + 5 + 5 = 21: open 1♠.

Rule of 15 (fourth seat, after three passes): add your HCP and your number of spades. With 15 or more, open; otherwise pass the hand out. Spades matter because the side with spades can outbid the other at the same level.

Quick tricks

Tricks you can take in the first two rounds of a suit, whoever plays the hand. They measure defence and how safe a hand is.

HoldingQuick tricks
AK2
AQ1½
A, or KQ1
Kx½

An opening bid usually has 2+ quick tricks. A preempt should have few (so you don't preempt with a good defensive hand), and a takeout double or strong opening should have plenty.

Losing Trick Count (LTC)

A way to value shapely hands with a fit, where points mislead. Count losers in each suit by looking only at its top three cards, counting each missing A, K or Q:

LengthLosers
Void0
SingletonA = 0; anything else = 1
DoubletonAK = 0; Ax or Kx = 1; Qx or xx = 2
3+ cardsAKQ = 0; AKx, AQx, KQx = 1; Axx, Kxx, Qxx = 2; xxx = 3

A normal opening bid has 7 losers. A minimum response with support (a single raise) has about 9; a limit raise has 8.

Tricks you can expect = 24 − (your losers + partner's losers).

Example: ♠AKJ10654 ♥10 ♦3 ♣10643 is only 11 HCP, but it has 6 losers (♠ 1, ♥ 1, ♦ 1, ♣ 3). Opposite a limit raise (8 losers): 24 − 14 = 10 tricks, so bid game.

LTC works only once you have an 8+ card trump fit, and it overstates hands with many queens and jacks. Use it as a check alongside points, not instead of them.

Counting winners (playing tricks)

For preempts and strong hands, count the tricks the hand will take with its own suit as trumps:

  • A long suit: count the cards, minus the missing top honours. KQJ10972 = 6 tricks (lose only the A). AQ109765 = about 5½.
  • Side suits: A = 1, AK = 2, KQ = 1.

A weak preempt promises tricks within 2 of the contract when vulnerable, 3 when not (the rule of 2 and 3): at the 3-level not vulnerable, about 6 playing tricks. A strong 2♣ opening with a long suit needs about 9 playing tricks.

Suit quality

Before overcalling or preempting in a suit, check it can stand being trumps. A quick test: length + honours (A, K, Q, J, 10) in the suit should be at least the number of tricks you are bidding for. A 2-level overcall contracts for 8 tricks, so KQ1084 (5 + 3 = 8) is enough, K8643 (5 + 1 = 6) is not.

Controls

For slam: an ace = 2 controls, a king = 1. The deck has 12. A slam needs first- or second-round control of every suit (an ace, a king, a void or a singleton), so the opponents can't cash two quick tricks.

Stoppers

A holding that stops the opponents running a suit in notrump:

  • Sure stopper: A; KQ; QJ10.
  • Likely stopper: Kx (if the lead comes up to it); Qxx; Jxxx.
  • Not a stopper: a singleton honour, Qx, Jxx.

Before bidding notrump in a competitive auction, look at their suit. If it isn't stopped, prefer a trump contract.

Part 2 · Contracts and scoring

How many points for each contract

Combined points of the partnership (HCP plus distribution), for a fair chance:

ContractTricksPoints needed
3NT925–26 (balanced hands)
4♥ / 4♠1025–26 with an 8-card fit (fewer with good shape)
5♣ / 5♦1128–29
Small slam (6)1233
Grand slam (7)1337

Below 25: a partscore. Between them: the bidding decides whether to invite. An invitation (e.g. 2NT) asks partner to bid game with the top of their range.

Choosing the strain: trumps or notrump

  • 8 cards between you in a suit is a fit. 9 or 10 is a great fit. 7 is not enough unless it is the best you have.
  • A major fit first. With an 8-card major fit, 4♥/4♠ usually takes one trick more than 3NT.
  • 3NT before 5 of a minor. 3NT needs 9 tricks, 5♣/5♦ needs 11. With a minor fit, look for 3NT unless a suit is wide open.
  • Balanced hands without a major fit belong in notrump.
  • Misfit (each of you short in the other's suit): stop low, and prefer notrump if you have stoppers.

Scoring basics

So 3NT, 4♥/4♠ and 5♣/5♦ are the cheapest games. Making a doubled contract scores double the trick value (and often turns a partscore into game) plus 50 "for the insult".

Vulnerability

Vulnerable, games and slams are worth more, and going down costs more. So: bid games more freely when vulnerable (at teams, bid a vulnerable game at about 40% odds, a non-vulnerable game at about 45%), and preempt and overcall more carefully. The best time to preempt is not vulnerable against vulnerable.

Teams (IMPs) and pairs (matchpoints)

  • Teams: what matters is the size of the gain or loss. Bid good games and slams, make your contract safely, don't double them into game for a small gain.
  • Pairs: you score against every other table, so +20 can matter. Overtricks matter, 3NT beats 4♥ when both make the same tricks, and you compete harder for partscores.

Part 3 · Bidding principles

Kinds of bids

TermMeaning
NaturalShows the suit you bid (or a notrump hand). 1♠ promising spades is natural.
Artificial / conventionalMeans something other than the suit named. Stayman 2♣ asks for a major; it says nothing about clubs. Opponents are told about these (alerts).
ForcingPartner must bid again. "Forcing for one round" or "game forcing" (keep bidding until game).
InvitationalAsks partner to bid game with a maximum and pass with a minimum.
Sign-off"Stop here." Partner should pass.
LimitedShows a narrow range (1NT = 15–17). Partner can now judge where to play.
UnlimitedCould be much stronger (a new suit by responder). Partner must keep the auction open.
PreemptiveA high bid on a weak hand with a long suit, to take bidding space from the opponents.
Cue-bidA bid of the opponents' suit (usually a strong raise or a strong takeout), or, near slam, a bid showing a control (an ace, king or shortness).

Who decides: the captain

Once one partner has made a limited bid, the other partner knows the combined strength best and becomes captain: they choose the contract. The limited hand describes; the captain decides. That is why after 1NT, responder makes the decisions.

Describing your hand

  • Longest suit first. With two 5-card suits, bid the higher-ranking first. With two 4-card suits, bid the cheaper first ("up the line").
  • Plan your rebid before you open (the principle of preparedness). If you can't rebid comfortably after the expected response, choose a different opening.
  • Bid each value once. Once you have shown your strength, don't bid it again; let partner decide.
  • Raise partner with support. Showing a fit early is the most useful thing you can tell partner.
  • A new suit by responder is forcing (in standard methods): it is unlimited.
  • Jumps show strength in constructive auctions, and weakness when preempting (weak jump overcalls, preemptive raises). Know which you play.
  • Fast arrival: in a game-forcing auction, jumping straight to game shows the weakest hand; going slowly leaves room for slam.
  • A reverse (opener's second suit higher-ranking than the first, at the 2-level) shows extra strength, because partner may have to go to the 3-level to give preference.

Doubles: penalty or takeout

A double can mean "I think they'll go down" (penalty) or "partner, bid one of the other suits" (takeout). The usual rule:

  • A double of a low-level suit bid, before partner has bid, is takeout.
  • A double of notrump, of a game or slam they bid freely, or after partner has bid notrump or we have found a fit and they've gone past it, is penalty (or "cooperative": partner may take it out with a very shapely hand).
  • Negative double: responder's double after an overcall is takeout, usually showing the unbid major.

When a double could be either, the partnership needs an agreement. Ours are in the System notes.

The Law of Total Tricks (LoTT)

Total tricks ≈ total trumps: the number of tricks both sides can take in their best fits is roughly the number of trumps they hold together in those fits. The practical rule:

In a competitive auction, it is safe to bid to the level equal to your side's number of trumps.

Your fitCompete to
8 cards2-level (8 tricks)
9 cards3-level (9 tricks)
10 cards4-level (10 tricks)

That is why a raise with 4-card support can be bid one level higher than one with 3. Adjust: bid less with a flat hand (4-3-3-3), honours in their suit, or vulnerable against not; bid more with shortness and a double fit.

"The 5-level belongs to the opponents": once they bid a game, outbidding them at the 5-level usually loses more than it gains, unless you are sure of 11 tricks.

Competitive judgment

  • Don't sell out at the 2-level when they have found a fit and the points are roughly even. Balance (bid or double in pass-out seat): partner has some values too.
  • Balancing borrows a king: in the pass-out seat, bid as if you held about 3 HCP more, and partner should allow for it (not bid again on the same values).
  • Penalty doubles of a partscore need trumps sitting over the declarer and a misfit with partner. Without trump tricks, compete instead.
  • Preempt early and to the limit: a preempt works best before the opponents have described their hands.

Part 4 · Declarer play

Plan before you play to trick 1

  1. Count. In notrump, count your sure winners (tricks you can take without giving up the lead). In a suit contract, count your losers from declarer's hand.
  2. Compare with what you need: winners needed minus winners you have, or losers you have minus losers you can afford.
  3. Find the extra tricks: establishing a long suit, finesses, ruffs in dummy, discarding losers.
  4. Check entries and danger. Can you reach the tricks you set up? Which opponent must not get the lead?
  5. Then play, starting with the step that must come first (usually drawing trumps, unless you need dummy's trumps for ruffing).

Notrump: winners and long suits

  • Establish your longest suit early, even at the cost of losing the lead, while you still have stoppers in their suit.
  • The hold-up: with A-x-x in the suit they lead, don't win the first trick or two. Hold up until the danger hand is out of cards in that suit. Rule of 7: subtract the number of cards you and dummy hold in their suit from 7; the answer is how many times to hold up.
  • Keep entries to the hand with the long suit. Play the high cards from the short hand first (unblock).

Suit contracts: losers and trumps

  • Draw trumps first, so the opponents can't ruff your winners, unless you need dummy's trumps for ruffing, or need a quick discard first.
  • Ruff in the short hand. Ruffing in dummy (usually the short trump hand) gains a trick; ruffing in the long trump hand gains nothing.
  • Discard losers on dummy's extra winners before giving up the lead.
  • Crossruff: when both hands have shortness, ruff back and forth, cashing side winners first.

Finesses

A finesse tries to win a trick with a card that is not the highest, hoping a missing honour is in the right place. Lead toward the card you hope will win.

  • AQ opposite xx: lead low toward the AQ and play the Q if the left-hand opponent plays low. Wins when the K is on your left (50%).
  • Kx opposite xx: lead toward the K. It wins when the A is on your left.
  • AQ10: a double finesse; finesse the 10 first, then the Q. Gains 2 tricks when both K and J are on the left.
  • Ruffing finesse: AKQJ opposite a void with trumps: lead the Q and discard if not covered; ruff if covered.
  • Two-way finesse: AJ3 opposite K109: you can finesse either opponent for the Q. Count the hand to choose.

Eight ever, nine never: missing the Q, with 8 cards between you, finesse; with 9, play the A and K hoping it falls.

More techniques

  • Ducking: deliberately lose an early round of a suit to keep an entry, or to set up the suit while dummy still has an entry.
  • Safety play: give up a possible overtrick to guard against a bad split, when making the contract is what matters.
  • Counting the hand: track how many cards each opponent has in each suit; by the middle of the hand you often know the shape, and the guess becomes certain.
  • Restricted choice: if an opponent plays one of two equal honours (Q or J), the other is now less likely to be with them. After you cash the A from AK1098 opposite 432 and one opponent drops the Q, finesse the other opponent for the J next time: with QJ they could have played either card, with a singleton Q they had no choice.
  • Endplay and squeeze: advanced ways to force an opponent to lead into your tenace, or to discard a winner. Recognise them by "I need the opponent to lead this suit" or "one opponent guards two suits".

Part 5 · Defence

Opening leads

Against notrump

  • Lead your longest and strongest suit, hoping to set it up: fourth best (the fourth card from the top) from a suit headed by an honour, KJ863 → 6.
  • With a sequence of three (KQJ, QJ10) or a broken one (KQ10), lead the top card.
  • Partner's suit if they bid one, usually.

Against a suit contract

  • Top of a sequence (KQJ → K) is the safest and best lead.
  • Partner's suit.
  • A singleton, hoping for a ruff, when you have trumps to ruff with and partner may have an entry.
  • Don't underlead an ace against a suit contract, and avoid leading away from Kx, Qx and other single honours into declarer's strength.
  • A trump lead when dummy will ruff and declarer's side is short in a suit you hold well.

The Rule of 11

When partner leads fourth best, subtract the card from 11: the answer is how many higher cards are in the other three hands. Subtract what you and dummy hold and you know how many declarer has.

Signals

SignalWhenStandard meaning
AttitudePartner leads a suit; you followHigh card = I like it, continue. Low = switch.
CountDeclarer leads a suitHigh-low = an even number; low-high = odd. Helps partner hold up or know when to win.
Suit preferenceWhen attitude and count don't matter (e.g. giving a ruff)A high card asks for the higher of the other suits; a low card, the lower.

Signal with cards you can spare. Your first discard is usually the clearest attitude signal you will give.

Defensive play

  • Second hand low: when declarer leads toward an honour, play low; let partner win the trick cheaply later.
  • Third hand high: when partner leads low, play your highest (or the lowest of equal high cards: from KQ7 play the Q).
  • Cover an honour with an honour when it may promote a lower card for your side (don't cover the first of touching honours from dummy).
  • Return partner's suit in notrump: the higher of two remaining cards, the original fourth best from four.
  • Don't give a ruff and discard (leading a suit both declarer and dummy are void in lets declarer ruff in one hand and discard a loser from the other).
  • Count declarer's tricks. If declarer has enough tricks to make once they get in, you must take your tricks now (cash out). If not, defend passively and wait.
  • Count declarer's points: from the bidding (1NT = 15–17) and the cards played, work out who holds the missing honours.
